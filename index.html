What Makes Technology “Civic”?

A1 — Civic Technology Analysis · Week 1 · Pietro Chiri · 11.458 Crowd Sourced City – Civic Tech Prototyping · Assignment 1 · Monday, September 14, 2026

Civil society has completely fallen out of love with the promise of a better future. The average American citizen is closer to bankruptcy than to ever becoming a millionaire, let alone a billionaire. The promise of the American dream has deflated completely, as homeownership, universal healthcare, accessible high education, and fair grocery prices seem impossible to obtain in the near future. In fact, a recent New York Times article compiles the opinions of several ten-year-olds interviewed on what life is going to be like by the year 2050. The answers are far from encouraging: bigger wealth gap, increasing cost of living, sparking wars, surviving rather than living. The political, economic, and moral crises of our current times call for meaningful action if we’re ever going to pull ourselves out of the post-pandemic tipping point onto the next quarter century.

Under these bleak circumstances, the authors of Power to the Public: The Promise of Public Interest Technology (2021) state the following: “There is no solving the world’s hardest problems without governments and institutions that really work for people”. And there lies the main issue: civil society has lost trust in their own democratic leaders who, originally meant to serve them, have rather pushed the middle class downwards while lobbying for their own financial interests. The authors Dawson McGuinness and Schank, however, decide to take a more optimistic approach in their set of core principles:

“Although … governments have failed, we believe deeply in government and the people who work in government at all levels. Government workers are not the problem. Government is not the problem. The problems lie in the systems, incentives, and structures government inhabits, which are no longer aligned well with serving its citizenry in a digital era.” (Preface p. xiv)

Whichever way technology is meant to serve communities at different scales, initiatives and techniques that promote the public good are certainly not new, dating far back the digital age (now the fast-pacing AI age). In Chapter 5, titled “How We Got Here and Where We’re Going,” the authors differentiate terminology for tools that essentially aim for governments to approach problem solving. Terms like “public interest technology,” “civic tech,” or “gov tech” are related phrases that practitioners may use indiscriminately, yet sprung in different historical times and may have responded to different needs.

On the one hand, “public interest technology” is defined by the authors as “the application of design, data, and delivery to advance the public interest and promote the public good in the digital age” (See Image 1). The term originates from law practice in the 1970s, when funders like the Ford Foundation saw a need for law school graduates to serve the public and fill in positions in the public sector. Likewise, a stronger public sector meant incorporating the innovative know-how of the private sector: effective use of technology based on data collection and analysis, high capacity to test and improve a minimum viable product, among others. Public interest technology builds on the notion of open government and open data, so that a government’s actions is available to everyone.

Terms like “civic tech,” on the other hand, overlaps and helps build the new field of public interest technology. Instead of heavily relying on cutting-edge technology (such as machine learning algorithms, cloud-based tools, etc.), public interest technology refers to the methodology that, though still technology-dependent, focuses more on the end users and progress tracking. The historical use of said terms also comes into play. Civic tech aimed to bring transparency and technology to governments as an early movement in the 1980s and 1990s, drawing from nonprofits organizations such as Computer Professionals for Social Responsibility and the Electronic Frontier Foundation. However, the new millennium and the democratization of internet access led to new ways in which an open government could be more transparent. Under the Obama administration, the 2009 Open Government Directive allowed for information to be publicly available online. As the authors state, open data at its core allows citizens to hold governments accountable in the digital age.

A recent encounter of mine with a public interest technology was during my summer internship with Slum Dwellers International in Cape Town, South Africa. SDI focuses on informal settlement upgrading across a network of 20+ countries in the Global South, with women and youth at the forefront of driving long-lasting change in their communities through data collection processes and negotiations with local governments. Recently, as part of the SDI Impact Innovation initiative, the Decision Labs consultancy is developing and operationalizing the City Data Platform for 7 pilot cities, as well as a cross-city dashboard (See Image 2). The platform aims to compile spatial quantitative and qualitative data on informal settlements in seven pilot cities in Africa. During part of my internship, I supported this taskforce with a user persona analysis. The purpose of the user persona analysis was to provide Decision Labs with insights into users’ needs, wants, and overall considerations, which have implications for the platform’s backend (data lake), the frontend (dashboard). The end users were the community members, the municipal officers, researchers in academia, and the SDI Network secretariat. Though it’s still a work in progress, I truly enjoyed the way the nonprofit organization was primarily thinking about the end users and the established synergy between community-collected data and novel technologies.
